# Complicated, Complex, and Cynefin

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish **complicated** work (many parts, cause and effect knowable in advance by experts) from **complex** work (cause and effect clearer after probes).
- Place a live slice in Snowden and Boone’s Cynefin contexts: clear, complicated, complex, chaotic, or disorder — as a **sense-making frame for the work**, not as a personality test.
- Match the decision pattern to the domain: sense-categorize-respond (clear), sense-analyze-respond (complicated), probe-sense-respond (complex), and act-sense-respond (chaotic).
- Split a sandwich project: keep a statute-shaped cutover on a complicated coat; keep a portal nobody has used on a complex coat.
- Refuse to treat Cynefin as a team-type quiz, a maturity ladder, or a moral ranking of “complex people.”
- Write a one-sentence classification that names the domain, the next honest move, and what would falsify the classification.
- Hand the classification to Lesson 3–4 so the loop (PDCA, OODA, BML, Double Diamond) matches the domain instead of the slide template.

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: the room can name diverge vs converge and will not score during generate.
- You can point at one live project that has a human at the end (a contractor, a payroll file, a user of a portal).
- If the work can harm people (safety, medical, legal, money that is not yours), classification does not replace a specialist review. A complex coat is not a license to probe a one-way door.

## Lesson Content

### Mechanism: what the distinction is for
**Hard** is not one thing. A tax packet with a published form and a filing date is hard the way a gearbox is hard: many parts, expertise, sequence, a wrong torque that still obeys physics. A contractor portal that nobody has used is hard the way a classroom is hard: people change each other while you work, and the story of cause and effect is cleaner **after** you act. The mechanism of this lesson is a classification that picks the next honest move. If you mis-file the work, you will write a specification for a user who does not exist, or you will prototype a statute.

The move is for the **work**, not for the **people**. Cynefin, as Snowden and Boone laid it out for leaders in [Harvard Business Review, November 2007](https://hbr.org/2007/11/a-leaders-framework-for-decision-making), is a sense-making frame for contexts. It is not a Myers-Briggs for teams. It is not a compliment. Calling a slice “complex” does not make you adaptive, modern, or Agile. Calling a slice “complicated” does not make you a dinosaur. The only question that pays is: **if I run the same steps next month, do I expect the same result?** Yes, with expertise and a checklist → complicated (or, if the pattern is obvious, clear). No, because the system talks back → complex. If the house is on fire → chaotic, and you act first.

Arc A’s job is to name the work and run a loop. Lesson 1 named the mode. This lesson names the **domain** of the work so Lesson 3–4 can pick a clock. A PDCA Check on a process you can observe is at home in complicated improvement. A probe you can afford to fail is at home in complex. Mixing them in one Gantt is how contractor onboarding becomes an eighteen-month “transformation” that still DMs staff to finish a W-4.

### Complicated vs complex, in working language
**Complicated** work has many parts, but the parts behave. An expert can decompose it. A checklist can finish it. Cause and effect exist in advance if you have the right expertise: known unknowns. Closing a known month-end, mapping a published API with tests, assembling a tax packet whose fields are specified by a statute, cutting over a payroll file format on a legal date — these reward sequence, rehearsal, and a definition of done. Waterfall-ish plans are not insults here. They are tools. Probing a statute “to learn” is delay dressed as humility.

**Complex** work has parts that change each other while you work. Cause and effect are clearer after you act: unknown unknowns. A new market, a new classroom, a new team habit, a portal nobody has used, the question of whether contractors will **find** the packet in the welcome mail — these reward small probes, not a 40-page Gantt that assumes the users already agreed. Expertise still matters. Expertise does not get to declare the cause-and-effect in advance without a probe. The honest next step is a safe-to-fail experiment, then sensing what happened, then responding.

Mislabeling is expensive in both directions. Treat complex work as complicated and you will freeze a guess in a specification, then call every mismatch a change request. Treat complicated work as complex and you will prototype a tax form that already has a statute, then call the delay “learning.” A practical pair of tells: (1) would a competent expert, given time to analyze, expect the same result next month? (2) is the failure reversible? If (1) is yes and (2) is no, you are probably in complicated with a one-way door — plan, rehearse, do not “MVP” the payroll file. If (1) is no and (2) is yes, you are probably in complex — probe.

### Cynefin is a sense-making frame, not a personality test
Snowden and Boone’s 2007 article, “A Leader’s Framework for Decision Making,” gives leaders five **contexts**: simple, complicated, complex, chaotic, and disorder. Later Cynefin teaching often names the first domain **clear** (it was also called obvious in between). This pack uses **clear** as the working name and notes that the HBR text says **simple**. Same domain: the relationship between cause and effect is obvious to most reasonable people; the move is sense, categorize, respond.

What the frame is for: matching a decision pattern to the context you are actually in, including the context of not knowing which context you are in (disorder). What it is not for: labeling people. There are no “complex personalities,” no “clear thinkers” as a caste, no badge for living in chaos. People have **comfort domains**. Under stress they retreat to the domain where they feel skilled — experts analyze, first-responders act, bureaucrats categorize. That retreat is a risk the 2007 article names. It is not a personality quiz you run in a retrospective with colored hats.

Do not teach Cynefin as a maturity ladder. Clear is not “dumb.” Complex is not “senior.” Chaotic is not “startup energy.” A fire alarm is chaotic for a minute and should not stay there as a management style. A published W-4 is clear or complicated and should not be probed as if it were a new market. Disorder is not a fifth personality. Disorder is the state of not having classified the work, which is the default of a meeting that starts with a portal mock.

If someone says “I’m a complex person, so I don’t do checklists,” they have turned a sense-making frame into an identity. Coats, not identities. The same operator who runs a complicated payroll cutover at 6 a.m. can run a complex findability probe at 2 p.m. Different coats. Same person. The wall from Lesson 1 still applies: name the mode. This lesson adds: name the **domain**.

### Clear: sense, categorize, respond
In the **clear** domain (HBR: simple), cause and effect are obvious. You sense the situation, categorize it, and respond with **best practice**. Known knowns. A contractor has not signed the packet; the packet is the published one; you send the link that always goes with that category. Process exists. Deviation is usually error, not innovation.

The failure catalog for clear is complacency and oversimplification. Best practice becomes unexamined. Entrained thinking: we always categorize this as a “welcome email,” so we never notice the subject line hides the tax packet. The 2007 article warns that a simple context can slip into chaotic when the category was wrong and the response was automatic. For onboarding: treating “everyone can find the packet because we emailed it” as a clear fact, when findability is actually complex, is how you get a chaotic Monday of Slack DMs and a missing filing.

Decision pattern: **sense–categorize–respond**. Do not run a workshop. Do not prototype the fire exit sign. Use a checklist. Use the published form. If you are tempted to “discover” a better W-4, you are mis-filing a clear or complicated legal object as a complex product bet.

### Complicated: sense, analyze, respond
In the **complicated** domain, cause and effect exist but are not obvious to everyone. You need expertise. Known unknowns. Several good answers may exist; this is the domain of **good practice**, not a single best practice. You sense, **analyze**, and respond. Analysis takes time. Experts may disagree. That disagreement is not complexity. It is expertise doing its job.

Payroll cutover on a legal date is the sandwich’s complicated slice. File formats, tax fields, identity-provider mapping, a dry run against a statute, a rollback plan if the file rejects. A competent payroll specialist, given time, can decompose this. A Gantt is not a sin. A rehearsal is not “Waterfall culture.” Skipping analysis because “we’re Agile now” is how you ship a file that cannot pay people.

The failure catalog for complicated is analysis paralysis, expert capture, and treating expert disagreement as a probe opportunity when you actually needed a decision rule among good practices. The other failure is importing a probe coat: “let’s MVP the cutover.” An irreversible statutory door is not a pretotype. Sense–analyze–respond, with a named Check (Lesson 3) on the dry run.

Decision pattern: **sense–analyze–respond**. Bring the expert. Write the sequence. Time-box the analysis so it cannot hide a complex slice (the portal) inside the same workstream. If the expert says “it depends on how contractors behave,” they just handed you a complex edge. Split it. Do not let the edge infect the file format.

### Complex: probe, sense, respond
In the **complex** domain, cause and effect are only obvious in retrospect. Unknown unknowns. Patterns emerge. The honest move is a **probe** that is safe to fail, then sense what happened, then respond. Emergent practice, not best practice and not yet good practice. You cannot analyze your way to a user who has not used the thing. You can watch five contractors try to find the tax packet and let that observation update the model.

The contractor **portal** — and, more tightly, **findability** and **pre-fill trust** — sit here until evidence says otherwise. Will a new contractor find the packet from the welcome mail on a phone? Will they trust a pre-filled field enough not to DM staff? Will a progress bar change behavior, or only the demo? These are not known unknowns you can assign to an expert to analyze in a room. They are unknown unknowns until a probe.

The failure catalog for complex is premature specification, a Gantt for a guess, and fake probes (a leadership demo that cannot fail, a survey about a portal nobody opened, an “experiment” whose success metric is stakeholder excitement). Another failure is staying in probe forever: lifestyle diverge from Lesson 1, now wearing a Cynefin hat. Probe–sense–respond still **responds**. After a probe, you may have earned a complicated slice (the packet is findable; now specify pre-fill against the HRIS). That is a domain shift, not a betrayal of complexity.

Decision pattern: **probe–sense–respond**. Small, reversible, instrumented. Lesson 4 will put Build–Measure–Learn on this coat. Lesson 3 will warn you not to skip Orient when the environment talks back. Do not probe the payroll file. Do probe the mail, the first screen, the moment of stuck.

### Chaotic and disorder
**Chaotic:** cause and effect are unclear; the immediate job is to stabilize. Decision pattern: **act–sense–respond**. Novel practice. Communication is a tool, not a workshop. A filing deadline in three hours, Slack on fire, the packet link 404s, contractors in three time zones cannot pay — you act to stop the bleeding (restore the link, staff the office hour, freeze the demo), then sense, then respond. Do not diverge. Do not analyze a gearbox while the house is on fire. Do not live here as a style. The 2007 article is explicit: once stability returns, you shift domains. Heroic chaos is not a strategy.

**Disorder:** you do not know which of the other four you are in. This is the most common meeting. People then default to their comfort domain: the engineer analyzes, the executive categorizes, the operator acts, the researcher probes, all on the same slide. The move in disorder is not to pick a favorite coat. It is to **break the situation into slices** you can classify. Payroll file over here (complicated). Findability over there (complex). 404 this morning (chaotic, then done). Welcome-mail template that has a known owner and a known edit path (clear). Disorder is unnamed work. Arc A exists to exit it.

Failure catalog for these two: staying chaotic as a badge (“we move fast”); using disorder as a reason to postpone classification; mixing all four slices on one Scrum board named “HR Digital” so the legal date hides behind story points. Also: treating a chaotic spike as proof that the whole program is complex, which is how you stop rehearsing the complicated cutover.

### Decision rule: the sandwich
Decision rule, not a personality test:

1. Ask: if we run the same steps next month, do we expect the same result? **Yes, obvious to a reasonable person** → clear → sense–categorize–respond. **Yes, with expertise** → complicated → sense–analyze–respond. **No, the system talks back** → complex → probe–sense–respond. **The house is on fire** → chaotic → act–sense–respond. **We cannot answer** → disorder → slice until you can.
2. Split sandwich projects in writing. Contractor onboarding is almost always a sandwich: **payroll / tax cutover is complicated**; **portal / findability / pre-fill behavior is complex**. Two clocks. Two definitions of done. Two loops. One program name is a lie if it hides the split.
3. Irreversible + expert-knowable → do not probe. Reversible + unknown-unknowns → do not Gantt as if the user already agreed.
4. Domain shifts are allowed and expected. A successful findability probe can earn a complicated implementation. A failed cutover rehearsal can reveal a complex training problem. Write the shift. Do not pretend the coat never changed.
5. If a person is using Cynefin to describe **themselves**, stop. Reclassify the **work**.

The sandwich is the whole practical yield. Leadership asked for one demo. The honest brief is two columns. Left: tax packet contents, file format, identity mapping, legal date — analyze, sequence, dry run, Check. Right: can a contractor find it, finish it unassisted, trust a pre-fill — probe, sense, respond. If you put both on one timeline called “portal launch,” the complicated column will be late and the complex column will be fake.

Write the split where a late arrival can see it: two columns, two clocks, two definitions of done. A useful tell that the rule was obeyed is that Thursday’s dry-run conversation cannot be hijacked by Wednesday’s demo frowns, and the findability probe cannot be postponed because the file layout is not yet pretty. If the two conversations keep collapsing into one, you are back in disorder and you have not yet named the work.

### Facilitation script: classify the work (30 minutes)
One facilitator. Wall from Lesson 1 still holds: this block is **converge on classification**, not generate of solutions. If people start pitching portals, park them.

**0:00–3:00 — Write the sandwich header.** Two columns: COMPLICATED / COMPLEX, plus a side box CHAOTIC / CLEAR / DISORDER. Rule on the wall: “We classify slices of work, not people.” Read it once.

**3:00–10:00 — Inventory slices, no coats yet.** Silent list of nouns the program currently mixes: tax packet fields, payroll file cutover, welcome email, findability, pre-fill, leadership demo, Slack DM load, identity provider, office hour staffing. One slice per line. No solutions.

**10:00–18:00 — Sort with the next-month test.** For each slice, the owner says yes/no to “same steps, same result next month?” and “reversible?” Facilitator places the card. Disagreement is useful: it often means the slice is still too coarse. Split “onboarding” into packet-contents vs findability rather than voting on a feeling.

**18:00–24:00 — Assign the decision pattern, not a vendor.** Clear → categorize/respond. Complicated → who analyzes, by when, what dry run. Complex → what probe this week, what would we sense. Chaotic → what act in the next hour (only if something is actually on fire). Disorder leftovers get split again or parked as “unnamed.”

**24:00–30:00 — Write the falsifiers and the two clocks.** For each placed card: what evidence would move it. “If 5/5 contractors find the packet from the current mail, findability is no longer the complex risk.” “If the dry-run file rejects, we stay in complicated analysis; we do not ‘iterate the statute.’” Close with two cadences: cutover rehearsal clock vs probe clock. The leadership demo is not allowed to merge them.

Cancel if a senior person insists on one coat for the whole program. A fake classification is worse than disorder, because it deletes the complicated rehearsal while claiming rigor.

### Failure catalog
**Personality Cynefin.** “Design is complex, engineering is complicated, ops is clear.” Those are identities. The tax packet does not care who holds it.

**Maturity ladder.** Treating complex as advanced and clear as shame. People then hide checklists and fake probes.

**One board, one domain.** Payroll cutover and portal probes in one stream named Agile. The legal date loses.

**Probe on a one-way door.** MVP of a payroll file. Pretotype of a dosing rule. Learning theater on an irreversible harm.

**Gantt on a guess.** Specification for a contractor who has not tried the mail. Change-request theater afterward.

**Comfort-domain retreat.** Under demo pressure, the expert analyzes the complex slice and the executive categorizes the complicated slice. Both are the 2007 warning. Re-sort the work, not the org chart.

**Permanent chaos.** Slack fire as a culture. Act–sense–respond used after the fire is out, which is just skip-analysis.

**Disorder as a mood.** “It’s all complex.” That sentence is how you refuse to slice.

**Domain pride.** Refusing to shift when a probe earned a specification, or when a rehearsal revealed a human pattern.

Uncertainty kept in the body: Cynefin has a life beyond the 2007 HBR article, including later names (obvious, clear) and a large consulting overlay. This pack teaches the **leadership decision patterns** as published in Snowden and Boone’s article and the professional move of matching a coat to a slice. It does not certify practitioners, settle later-framework disputes, or recruit vendor methods.

## Worked example(s)
**Problem:** The 20/15 from Lesson 1 kept three clusters for contractor onboarding: (A) findability of the tax packet, (B) pre-fill / fewer fields, (C) office hour so DMs die. Leadership still wants one Friday demo of “the portal.” Payroll has a legal cutover date for the file that feeds any future pre-fill. The identity provider is known. Slack is not on fire this morning, but last Monday it was, when the packet link 404d. The program is currently one board: HR Digital.

**Steps:**
1. Exit disorder by slicing. Write cards: payroll file cutover; tax-packet field contents; identity-provider mapping; welcome-email findability; pre-fill trust; leadership demo; Slack 404 response; office-hour staffing.
2. Next-month test. File cutover, field contents, identity mapping: a competent payroll/tax expert expects the same result if the statute is unchanged → **complicated**. Findability and pre-fill trust: running “launch portal” next month does not expect the same contractor behavior → **complex**. A known 404 playbook: **clear** once written. Last Monday’s 404 in the first hour: **chaotic** then, not now. Office-hour staffing: complicated scheduling, complex only if you still do not know whether humans are the actual need.
3. Sandwich on the wall. Left column (complicated): payroll cutover, field mapping, identity, dry run, rollback. Pattern: sense–analyze–respond. Right column (complex): findability probe, then pre-fill trust probe. Pattern: probe–sense–respond. Do not put them on one Gantt named portal.
4. Refuse personality talk. Ops is not “the complicated people.” Design is not “the complex people.” The same trio can rehearse a file at 10:00 and watch a contractor find a mail at 14:00.
5. Assign clocks. Cutover: rehearsal dates, expert analysis, a Check on file accept/reject (Lesson 3). Findability: a probe this week with five new contractors and a task-success count, not a portal mock. Pre-fill stays behind findability; you cannot trust a field you cannot find.
6. Place the leadership demo. A demo that cannot fail is not a complex probe. Either the Friday artifact is a **report of the probe** (honest) or it is a **clickable costume** (disorder wearing makeup). The sandwich says: show the mail-findability result; do not show a portal that implies cutover is already a product.
7. Write falsifiers. If 5/5 contractors find the packet in the current mail, findability leaves complex and the next risk (pre-fill trust) can be probed. If the dry-run file rejects, stay in complicated analysis; do not “iterate” the statute in a design studio.

**Answer / result:** The work now has two named coats. **Payroll cutover is complicated.** **Portal/findability is complex.** The Friday artifact is allowed to be a findability result plus a cutover status, and is not allowed to be a single portal story. Lesson 3 can put PDCA on the packet-completion process once a process exists to Check, and OODA on the moving demo environment if leadership’s date keeps jumping. Lesson 4 can write Learn before Build on the complex column only. The sandwich is the name of the work.

## Key Terms
| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| Complicated work | Many parts; cause and effect knowable in advance with expertise; good practice |
| Complex work | Parts change each other; cause and effect clearer after probes; emergent practice |
| Clear (HBR: simple) | Cause and effect obvious; sense–categorize–respond; best practice |
| Chaotic | Cause and effect unclear; act–sense–respond to stabilize; do not live here |
| Disorder | Not knowing which domain the slice is in; default meeting state until you slice |
| Sense–analyze–respond | Complicated decision pattern: expertise, decomposition, then action |
| Probe–sense–respond | Complex decision pattern: safe-to-fail experiment, then read the system, then respond |
| Sense–categorize–respond | Clear decision pattern: match to a known category and apply best practice |
| Act–sense–respond | Chaotic decision pattern: stabilize first, then take stock |
| Sandwich project | One program that mixes a complicated core with a complex edge; must be split in writing |
| Domain shift | Reclassification after evidence (probe earned a spec; rehearsal revealed a human pattern) |
| Comfort domain | The context a person retreats to under stress; a risk, not a type |

## Misconception inventory
| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| “Cynefin types our team.” | It classifies **contexts of work**. People are not domains. |
| “Complex is more evolved than complicated.” | There is no maturity ladder. A statute is not junior. A portal is not senior. |
| “If it’s hard, it’s complex.” | Gearboxes are hard and complicated. Classrooms are hard and complex. Hard is not the distinction. |
| “Experts make work complicated, so skip them and probe.” | Complicated work **needs** experts. Skipping analysis on a payroll file is harm. |
| “Agile means everything is complex.” | Agile values late-breaking information. A legal cutover can still be sequenced. |
| “We should MVP the payroll cutover.” | Irreversible statutory doors are not pretotypes. Sense–analyze–respond, with rehearsal. |
| “A Gantt will handle the portal if we add more detail.” | A Gantt assumes knowable cause and effect. Findability needs a probe first. |
| “Chaos means we are moving fast.” | Chaos is a short stabilize-first context. Living there is skipped Orient plus skipped analysis. |
| “Disorder means wait.” | Disorder means slice until you can classify. Waiting is unnamed work. |
| “Once complex, always complex.” | Domains shift. A successful probe can earn a complicated implementation. |

## Summary
- Complicated: experts, analysis, checklists, good practice. Complex: probes, emergence, safe-to-fail experiments.
- Cynefin (Snowden and Boone, HBR 2007) is a sense-making frame for work contexts, not a personality test or a maturity ladder.
- Clear: sense–categorize–respond. Complicated: sense–analyze–respond. Complex: probe–sense–respond. Chaotic: act–sense–respond. Disorder: slice.
- Sandwich: payroll/tax cutover is complicated; portal/findability is complex. Two clocks, two definitions of done.
- Do not probe a one-way door. Do not Gantt a guess.
- Classify slices, write falsifiers, allow domain shifts.
- Named work is the input to a named loop.

## Practice
### Retrieval
- Give the next-month test that separates complicated from complex.
- Name the decision pattern for clear, complicated, complex, and chaotic.
- What is disorder, and what is the first move in it?
- Why is Cynefin not a personality test? Give one sentence you would put on the wall.
- In the onboarding sandwich, which slice is complicated and which is complex, and why?
- What is a domain shift, with one onboarding example?

### Near transfer
A program board titled “HR Digital” contains: (1) a payroll file cutover with a legal date, (2) a new contractor portal, (3) a 404 that is happening right now, (4) a welcome-email template with a known owner. Classify each. What goes wrong if they share one sprint review?

### Far transfer
A hospital wants to “Agile” both a published dosing checklist and a new family-waiting app. Map the sandwich. Which slice is not allowed to be a probe, and what Cynefin misuse would let a team skip the checklist because they identify as “complex people”?

## Spaced retrieval notes
- Day 0: classify one live project as complicated or complex in one sentence, with the next-month test.
- Day 2: find a sandwich and split it in two columns (including one onboarding-like mix of statute and behavior).
- Day 7: catch one Cynefin-as-personality use (in the wild or in your own mouth) and reclassify the work.
- Day 21: write a domain shift you actually observed — a probe that earned a spec, or a plan that hit a human pattern.

## Difficulty tiers
- **Novice:** You can tell complicated from complex with the next-month test and you will not call people “a Cynefin type.”
- **Working:** You can slice a sandwich, assign decision patterns, and keep two clocks without merging them for a demo.
- **Expert:** You exit disorder in the room, write falsifiers, allow domain shifts, and refuse probes on one-way doors while still probing the complex edge.

## Domain scaffolds
Professional building work: write the domain on the brief next to the mode (Lesson 1). Safety-critical and statute-shaped slices stay checklist-first even when the product around them is complex. Contractor onboarding is the continuing sandwich: tax packet / payroll cutover as complicated analysis; findability and pre-fill as complex probes; leadership demo as a temptation to merge them. Do not let the demo pick the domain.
