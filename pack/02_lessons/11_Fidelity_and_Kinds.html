Fidelity and Kinds of Prototype

Learning Objectives

  • Treat fidelity as an attention budget: spend finish only on the question you can name, not on the stakeholder’s appetite for polish.
  • Choose low, mid, or high fidelity by what the cheaper rung still cannot kill, and refuse to climb because someone asked for a “real demo.”
  • Tell looks-like, works-like, and experience prototypes apart, and match the kind to the risk (appearance, mechanism, or the episode in time).
  • Brief a vice president on paper when paper is the honest rung, by naming the question the paper is for.
  • Run a short facilitation to name the question, pick a kind, pick a rung, and write the refuse-the-climb sentence.
  • Recognize a high-fidelity artifact that cannot fail as a sales object, not a test.
  • Hand L12 a kind and a rung, not a pretty shell of the wrong risk.

Prerequisites

  • L08: a brief with purpose, end state, constraints, and a How Might We. Fidelity without a question is costume.
  • L10: at least one testable claim, clustered by mechanism — who, action, measure. If you only have slogans, do not prototype; return to progression.
  • L01 / L04: you can tell a probe from a plan. A prototype is a probe. A polished tour of a future product is usually a plan pretending to be a probe.
  • You can name one assumption that, if false, should stop the work. L12 will rank assumptions; this lesson decides how finished the artifact is allowed to look and which kind of artifact it is.

Lesson Content

What the move is for

Fidelity is how finished the artifact looks or behaves. It is not a virtue. It is an attention budget. Every hour you spend on polish, motion, branding, or a real data path is an hour you did not spend on the question. The move is for matching the budget to the question so the room can still kill the idea. Low fidelity is a kindness: it is easier to kill. High fidelity is a commitment of attention, and attention is what teams run out of before they run out of opinions.

The injury is using high fidelity too early. Polish persuades. Stakeholders say yes to a picture of a product that has not been tested. Then the picture becomes the spec, the spec becomes the quarter, and the quarter becomes a hostage situation. You did not learn. You decorated a guess. Build–Measure–Learn in the wrong intellectual order (build first because building is comforting) is this injury with a blog vocabulary.

This lesson does not yet choose pretotype versus prototype versus MVP. Those are L12’s names for how real the test is — demand before mechanism, a failing artifact, or a shipped measured slice. This lesson chooses how finished and which kind (looks, works, experience). Get these wrong and L12 will honestly test the wrong thing.

Decision rule: climb only when the cheaper rung cannot kill the assumption

Write the assumption as a question. “Can a new contractor even find the packet?” “Does pre-fill from HRIS reduce typing without lying?” “Will a tired person finish at 11 p.m.?” Then ask: what is the cheapest artifact that can make this question false? Stay on that rung. Climb one step only when you have evidence the cheaper rung cannot speak — not when a stakeholder is bored with paper, not when a designer is bored with boxes, not when a demo slot appeared on a leadership calendar.

Stay low when the question is “is this even the job?” or “do they understand the path?” Paper, boxes, a script, a map of the current maze. Move mid when the question is “can a person get through the steps?” and paper cannot hold the sequence — clickable wireflows, a Wizard-of-Oz backend (a human pretending to be the system), structured but unfinished. Move high when the question is texture: timing, trust, accessibility, load, the feeling of a real identity provider. High is for the last unknowns that look and feel were hiding, not for impressing a room.

If you cannot name the question, you may not climb. If the question is legal, you may not prototype the law; you ask counsel, then pick a rung for the remainder. If the question is mechanism and you are drawing a pretty shell, you are on the wrong kind, not only the wrong rung. Kind and rung are independent knobs. Twist both on purpose.

Low, mid, and high — three rungs, not a career ladder

Low-fidelity is cheap and ugly on purpose. Paper screens, cardboard, a spoken script, a spreadsheet pretending to be a form, a printed map of links. It answers job and comprehension questions. It fails well: people will scribble on it. It fails badly only when the facilitator apologizes for it, which trains the room to wait for polish.

Mid-fidelity is structured but unfinished. The steps exist. The type is readable. The path can be clicked or walked. The backend may be fake. It answers sequence questions: where do they get stuck, which label is a lie, which step is extra. Mid is the honest home of most product probes. Teams skip it because it is not cute and not “real.” That skip is how they jump from a slogan to a high-fidelity hostage.

High-fidelity looks or works close to the real thing — visual texture, motion, real auth, real latency, real copy in the brand voice. It answers questions that only the real texture can ask: do they trust it, can they do it on a small phone in bad light, does the load fail, does accessibility hold, does a 12-second spinner kill completion. If you do not have one of those questions, high fidelity is costume jewelry. It will attract expert review (L14) of a thing that was never claiming to be done, and you will spend a week fixing the chrome of a wrong job.

Rungs are not identity. A senior designer can run paper. A junior engineer can run high on a works-like spike. “We are a high-fidelity team” is a coat you should take off for the first question. Climbing is not promotion. Climbing is what you do when the current rung is mute.

Looks-like, works-like, experience

A looks-like prototype tests appearance, hierarchy, and recognition: will they know what this is, can they find the primary action, does the layout match the job. It may be paper or high-visual. Looks-like is honest when the risk is “they will not recognize the packet” or “the hierarchy hides the submit action.” It is fraud when the risk is “the matcher does not work” and you still spent the week on shadows.

A works-like prototype tests mechanism: the algorithm, the join, the data path, the mechanical hinge, the pre-fill mapping from HRIS. It may look like junk. Junk is in spec. A works-like that you pretty up “so leadership can see it” often stops being able to fail, because now it is a demo. If mechanism is the risk, the ugly spike is the professional object.

An experience prototype tests the whole episode in time: waiting, handoff, emotion, the phone call after, the 11 p.m. attempt on a phone after a full day of contracting. Wizard-of-Oz, concierge, a rehearsal of the real email arriving in a real inbox — these are experience moves. Teams often build looks-like when the risk is experience (will a tired person complete this) or works-like (does the join hold). Name which one you are making. A pretty shell with no mechanism is not “agile.” It is a looks-like. Allowed if appearance is the risk. Fraud if the risk was mechanism.

You can mix kinds only if you can still say which risk is on trial. A mid-fidelity experience test with a junk works-like underneath is coherent. A high looks-like with a slide that claims the algorithm is “in progress” is a costume. L12 will decide whether the mix is a pretotype (demand/comprehension before mechanism), a prototype (can fail), or an MVP (shipped). L11 only demands that you not lie about the kind.

Match the question, not the rank

A vice president can read paper if you tell them what question the paper is for. If they cannot, you have an alignment problem (L08), not a fidelity problem. Rank does not buy a right to consume attention. A leadership demo slot is a calendar fact; it is not a reason to climb. The sentence you practice is: “This paper is for can they find the packet. A polished helper would answer a different question we are not yet allowed to spend.”

If they still need polish, you are demoing, not testing. Rename it. A demo can be a political necessity. It does not get to call itself a prototype. Do not let a demo steal the probe’s name, budget, or kill rule. Run the paper test on the claim; if you must, take a separate looks-like to the demo with a caption that says it is a picture, not evidence.

The same rule runs downward. Do not hide paper from users because it “looks unprofessional.” Users, told the question, will usually play. Users, shown a high-fidelity lie, will comment on the chrome and hide the job failure to be polite. Fidelity is an attention budget for them too. High fidelity spends their attention on your taste.

Failure catalog

Fidelity failures are almost always social. The artifact did what artifacts do. The room used it to avoid a kill.

  • Climbing for a demo slot. The calendar became the brief.
  • Looks-like when the risk is works-like. Pretty matcher that does not match.
  • Works-like that cannot fail because it was prettied for leadership and now “has to go well.”
  • Experience risk tested with screenshots — no time, no inbox, no 11 p.m., no handoff.
  • Apologizing for paper, which trains everyone to wait for the hostage.
  • “We are a high-fidelity culture.” Identity where a rung should be.
  • High-fi inviting heuristic review too early (Nielsen’s list is a L14 expert overlay). You will sand the chrome of the wrong job.
  • Skipping mid because it is not cute and not real. Mid is where sequence dies or lives.
  • One artifact asked to be all three kinds. Then nothing is on trial.

If the artifact cannot make the named question false, it is not on a rung. It is a poster. Stop spending.

What not to spend fidelity on

Do not spend high fidelity on a claim whose riskiest unknown is still “do they want this” or “do they understand this.” That spend belongs to a cheap pretotype (L12), often low-experience or low-looks. Do not spend works-like engineering on a path nobody can find. Do not spend motion design on a Wizard-of-Oz that is testing sequence. Do not spend brand on a spike.

Do not prototype a one-way safety door as a cute fake if the fake could teach a dangerous action — L12 will make that a hard rule. From a fidelity view, the point is the same: finish is not innocence. A high looks-like of an unsafe pattern is a better teacher of the unsafe pattern. Stay low, or do not make it, or make it with the safety owner in the room and a script that cannot be mistaken for the real door.

Do not use users as unpaid labor to pretty a mechanism you already know is broken. That is not a test. That is extracting patience. If the mechanism is known-broken, fix or kill it; do not climb.

Facilitation script: twenty-five minutes to name the rung

This is a decision meeting, not a design studio. The artifact, if any, is whatever you already have from L10. The output is a filled card: question, kind, rung, refuse-the-climb sentence, what would make us climb later. If leadership is in the room, they are here to hear the question, not to request chrome.

Put a blank ladder on the wall (low / mid / works-like / high) and three kind labels (looks / works / experience). Put the L10 claim at the top. Then run the clock. The facilitator’s only job is to keep people from jumping to “we should just build it in the real stack so it’s real.” Real is L12’s MVP, and only after a cheaper rung is mute.

Minute-by-minute:

  • 0:00–0:05 — Write the question. Turn the claim into one interrogative. “Can they find and start the packet in five minutes, unassisted?” If two questions appear, you have two tests; pick one for this artifact. If nobody can write the question, stop. You are not ready to spend fidelity.
  • 0:05–0:10 — Kind. Vote with speech, not dots: is the unknown appearance, mechanism, or the episode in time? For contractor onboarding, findability is mostly experience (the episode of hunting) plus a little looks-like (recognition). Pre-fill quality is works-like. A brand-new helper visual is looks-like, and is probably the wrong kind this round.
  • 0:10–0:15 — Cheapest rung that can kill it. For findability: a paper map of the current email → login → zip → form, walked in real time, is enough. A Figma of a future helper cannot kill findability. For pre-fill: a Wizard-of-Oz paste (human as HRIS) is mid experience/works-like, still not high. Mark the rung on the ladder.
  • 0:15–0:20 — Refuse-the-climb sentence. Write it in the first person the room will actually say: “We are not polishing a helper until findability fails or holds on paper.” If a VP is present, say it to them now. If they need a picture for a different meeting, schedule a demo with that caption, separately.
  • 0:20–0:25 — Climb-later rule. One sentence: “We climb to mid when three of five cannot be understood on paper, or when paper cannot hold the sequence.” Without that sentence, the next calendar invite will climb for free.

Send the card. Do not send renderings unless the chosen kind/rung is a rendering. A looks-like of the wrong risk is worse than no picture.

Hand-off to the smallest honest test

L11 names kind and rung. L12 names how real the trial is (pretotype, prototype, MVP) and writes the four-part slice: audience, task, fail rule, STOP. Do not skip the hand-off. A beautiful paper map with no fail rule is still a poster. A shipped helper with no named kind is still a costume. The two lessons share one climb rule: do not spend the next rung, and do not ship, until the cheaper honest object can no longer speak.

Write one sentence on the card that L12 is not allowed to renegotiate: “Kind = experience; rung = low; question = can they find and start the packet.” If Friday’s politics demand a picture, that picture is a demo with a caption, scheduled beside the test, never instead of it. Arc C’s job is a claim that can still die. Fidelity is how little death-of-the-claim you spend before you find out.

Uncertainty: “Fidelity” in product work is a field term with no single canonical paper in this syllabus. Low/mid/high and looks/works/experience are teaching knobs, not ISO grades. Adjacent rungs blur; the decision rule does not. If you can still kill the assumption cheaper, you are too high.

Worked example(s)

Problem: L10 promoted Claim 1: a new contractor can start the required packet within five minutes of opening the offer email, without DMing staff. Claim 2 (pre-fill) is parked as downstream. The director still wants a “realistic demo” of the AI helper for Friday’s leadership meeting. Design has a mid-fidelity Figma of the helper. Engineering asks whether to spike HRIS mapping this sprint. The question nobody has written down is findability.

Steps:

  1. Write the question for Claim 1. “Can a new contractor find and start the packet within five minutes of the offer email, unassisted?” That is not “does the helper look trustworthy,” and not “does HRIS map.”
  2. Pick kind. The unknown is the episode: email, logins, zip, form, time, fatigue. Experience first. Looks-like branding is a non-goal. Works-like pre-fill is Claim 2.
  3. Pick the cheapest rung. A low-fidelity experience probe: paper (or literal) map of the current path, five new contractors, think-aloud, facilitator does not help unless they stop for two minutes. Real inbox if possible. No new UI. The Figma helper cannot kill this question; it can only persuade Friday.
  4. Refuse the climb. Sentence: “Friday can have a captioned picture if politics require a demo. The picture is not the test. We do not spike HRIS until findability holds or fails on this rung.”
  5. Climb-later rule. If 3/5 cannot even start within five minutes, do not climb; fix findability (L12’s fail rule will use this). If they can start, then a Wizard-of-Oz pre-fill (human pastes HRIS values) is the next mid works-like/experience hybrid — still not high, still not an MVP.
  6. Name the Figma honestly. It is a looks-like of help-at-stuck, the L10 cluster you did not promote. Allowed as a separate demo object. Forbidden as “the prototype.”

Answer / result: Attention stays on the maze that already exists. The helper is not allowed to spend the sprint. A VP who needs paper explained gets the question in one sentence; if they still demand polish, you have a demo request, not a fidelity need. Friday can see a captioned looks-like if politics require it — labeled picture, not evidence — and the five contractors still walk the current email → login → zip path on paper or in a real inbox. Engineering’s HRIS spike waits on the climb-later rule. L12 will add audience, fail rule, and STOP, and will rank findability above pre-fill. L11’s job was to keep the budget on the rung that can still kill Claim 1, and to keep the director’s helper from renaming itself “the prototype.”

Summary

  • Fidelity is an attention budget. Spend it on the question.
  • Low / mid / high are rungs. Climb only when the cheaper rung cannot kill the assumption.
  • Looks-like, works-like, and experience answer different risks. Name the kind.
  • A vice president can read paper if you name the question; if they cannot, you have an alignment problem.
  • A high-finish object that cannot fail is a sales artifact. Rename it.
  • Do not spend finish on the wrong kind, on a known-broken mechanism, or on a safety pattern that teaches a dangerous action.
  • Facilitate a 25-minute card: question, kind, rung, refuse-the-climb sentence, climb-later rule.
  • Hand L12 a kind and a rung, not a pretty shell of the unchosen cluster.

Practice

Retrieval

  • What question does each rung (low, mid, high) honestly answer?
  • Looks-like vs works-like vs experience, in one sentence each.
  • State the climb rule in one sentence.
  • What do you say to a VP who wants polish when paper is the honest rung?
  • Why is MVP not the top of this lesson’s ladder?
  • Name two fidelity failures that are social rather than technical.

Near transfer

Stakeholders want a “realistic demo” next week. The riskiest unknown is legal, not visual. What kind and rung do you make, what do you ask counsel, and what do you refuse to build?

Far transfer

You are testing this Atlas pack with mixed-knowledge learners. What is a low-fidelity experience probe of “can they find Lesson 11 from the cover,” and what high-fidelity spend would be a lie about that question?

Spaced retrieval notes

  • Day 0: list three live assumptions; write the cheapest rung and kind that could kill each.
  • Day 2: write a refuse-the-climb sentence for one project and say it out loud to someone senior, or practice it.
  • Day 7: run or observe a lo-fi test; note whether polish snuck in, and whether the kind matched the risk.
  • Day 21: catch a demo that could not fail; rename it sales; check whether it stole budget from a mute cheaper rung.

Difficulty tiers

  • Novice: lo / mid / hi; looks vs works vs experience; paper is legal.
  • Working: write the question; pick kind and rung; write climb-later.
  • Expert: hold a VP to paper when paper is the honest rung; split a mixed artifact into a demo and a probe without poisoning either.

Domain scaffolds

Professional products: disclose when an experience probe is Wizard-of-Oz or concierge, especially if trust is the product. Safety-critical: a high looks-like of a dangerous control is a teaching hazard; stay low or do not make it (L12’s one-way door rule). Accessibility is a high-rung question and a constraint: do not wait for high-fi to ask whether a packet can be completed with a keyboard and a reader — but do not use that as an excuse to build the whole product before findability is tested. Complicated compliance (tax, identity): the form may already be statutory (complicated, checklist); the path to the form is often complex and deserves a low experience probe, not a high looks-like of a helper.
