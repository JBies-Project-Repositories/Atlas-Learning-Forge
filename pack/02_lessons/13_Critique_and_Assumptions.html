Critique, RAT, and Pre-mortem

Learning Objectives

  • Cancel a critique that has no brief and keep the artifact on trial, not the author.
  • Run I like / I wish / What if as a timed speech protocol that can change the next spend.
  • Map assumptions on ignorance × impact and treat a riskiest-assumption test as a stop/go gate.
  • Run a pre-mortem that produces owned failure stories rather than gallows humor.
  • Steelman the strongest fair case for killing the favorite idea before keeping it.
  • End every review with a named move: redesign, solidify, kill, pivot, or ship.
  • Facilitate a 35-minute critique without turning it into a status meeting or a live redesign.

Prerequisites

  • Lesson 8: a brief exists — purpose, end state, constraints, success criteria. If it does not, this lesson’s first move is cancel.
  • Lessons 11–12: some artifact exists (paper, pretotype, prototype, or increment) and at least one assumption has been named.
  • You can sit silent while other people are specific about your work for twenty minutes.

Lesson Content

The artifact is on trial, not the author

A design critique is a structured look at an artifact against the brief. It is not a status meeting, not a round of applause, and not a roasting. The artifact is on trial. The author is a witness with a later turn. If the room starts defending a person, or attacking a person, the critique has already failed its job. Rank, hours spent, and how pretty the file is are all out of scope.

The move is FOR changing the next spend while the spend is still cheap. You look together so that one person’s blind spot does not become the plan. You do it in public because hallway vetoes are how artifacts die after the calendar says they lived. A critique that cannot change the next week is a salon. Salons produce quotes. Gates produce decisions.

Decision rule: if you cannot point at the artifact and the brief in the same glance, you are not critiquing. You are having feelings in a conference room. Put the brief on the wall. Put the artifact next to it. Speak only in sentences that touch both.

The usual injuries are two mirrors of each other. Kindness without specificity (“this is great, maybe tweak the color”) leaves the author with no move. Cruelty without a brief (“I just don’t like it”) is taste dressed as leadership. Both waste the slot. Specificity is the kindness: “Against the end state ‘complete the packet unassisted,’ this first screen does not name the packet, so a tired contractor will bounce.” That sentence can be used on Monday. “Make it pop” cannot.

Cancel if there is no brief

People will critique their imaginary product. They will fill missing purpose with their last job, missing end state with polish, and missing constraints with whatever is fashionable this quarter. The meeting will feel productive because everyone spoke. The artifact will not have been tried against anything that can fail. You will walk out aligned to a ghost.

Cancel is a professional move. It is not drama. The facilitator says: “We do not have purpose, end state, and constraints on one page. We are not looking at this for thirty-five minutes. We will write the brief (Lesson 8) and reconvene.” If a senior person insists on “just a quick look,” they are asking for a hallway veto with extra chairs. Note it and still cancel. A fake critique trains the team that review is theater, and theater is cheaper than a test until the invoice arrives.

If the brief exists but the author has not stated the question the artifact is supposed to answer, you are one step from a tour. A tour is a sales demo. “Walk us through the flow” is how a looks-like prototype becomes the spec. The question is the gate: “Does a new contractor find the tax packet from the onboarding email in five minutes without help?” Now the room knows what to like, wish, and what-if against. Without that sentence, I like becomes taste, I wish becomes a new product, and What if becomes a second brainstorm.

Write the question at the top of the agenda, under the brief. If the author cannot write it in one sentence, they are not ready to be reviewed. They need a smaller honest test (Lesson 12), not an audience. An audience will help them decorate.

I like / I wish / What if is a speech protocol

I like / I wish / What if is a simple protocol that keeps the room in useful speech. I like is specific appreciation tied to the brief, not a personality compliment. I wish is a desired change, still tied to the brief, not a new epic. What if is a proposed experiment, not a second diverge session. Ban “I don’t like it” without a brief-linked reason. Ban silent senior people who later veto in a hallway. Ban the author arguing during the round. Ban “as a user” from people who are not doing the job.

The protocol is FOR separating noticing from deciding. During the round, the job is to put information on the table. Deciding is a later five minutes. Mixing them is how the first senior “I wish” becomes the redesign. Deferred judgment (Lesson 9) still applies inside a critique: you are not generating a hundred options, but you are not scoring in real time either. The author listens. The room does not vote the pixels.

Decision rule: every speech must name (1) a part of the artifact, (2) a part of the brief, and (3) one of the three stems. “I like that the packet title is in the email subject, because the end state is unassisted findability.” Pass. “I like the energy.” Fail — no artifact, no brief. “What if we also built a chatbot?” Fail unless it is an experiment on the current question that could run this week. In critique, What if is a test, not a new How Might We.

Time-box the round. One facilitator. The author listens first, then answers questions, then decides what to change. A critique that becomes a live redesign by committee is a second brainstorm without deferred judgment. The author owns the artifact after the meeting. The room owns the quality of the speech during it. If the author is also the highest-paid person in the room, appoint a facilitator who can cut them. Otherwise the protocol is a costume.

Facilitation: a 35-minute critique agenda

This agenda is the mechanism. It is not a suggestion. If you cannot protect the clock, you cannot protect the artifact. One facilitator, not the author. The facilitator’s only job is the protocol, the brief, and the end-move. They do not add a surprise “I wish” of their own unless they have declared they are a reviewer for this slot. Dual-hatting is how the clock dies.

Put the brief and the question on the wall or the first slide. Paper beats a twelve-slide walkthrough. Everyone can see the same three sentences. Phones down is not a culture speech; it is how you keep the round from becoming Slack with extra latency. If someone must take notes, they take notes on speech-to-brief links, not on who “won.”

ClockMove
0:00–0:03Facilitator reads purpose, end state, constraints, and the question. If any of the first three is missing, cancel.
0:03–0:06Author states the question in one sentence and where to look. No feature tour. Sixty seconds of silent looking.
0:06–0:20Round-robin I like / I wish / What if. Author silent. Facilitator cuts speeches that lack a brief link. Park live redesigns on a later list.
0:20–0:25Clarifying questions only. Author may answer facts (“yes, that button is dead”). No defense speeches.
0:25–0:30Author responds: what they heard, what they will change, what they will not. The room does not vote the design.
0:30–0:35Named end-move: redesign, solidify, kill, pivot, or ship. Write it on the brief. If the riskiest assumption is still untested, the move cannot be ship.

Decision rule for the facilitator: when the clock hits 0:30 and nobody has a move, you offer the stalling sentence back to them — “Keep thinking is not on the list” — and you wait. Silence is cheaper than a fake solidify. If the room is larger than eight, split: a core of people who will spend next week on this artifact, plus a note-taker. A critique of twenty is a town hall. Town halls produce vibes.

Do not bolt a pre-mortem, a stakeholder briefing, and a user test into the same 35 minutes. This agenda is one look at one artifact. Assumption mapping and RAT can take the last five minutes only if the fatal unknown is already written. If it is not, schedule them. Compression is how “we reviewed” becomes a lie.

Assumption mapping: ignorance × impact

Assumption mapping puts claims on two axes: how much you know, and how bad it is if you are wrong. The upper-right (unknown, fatal) is where Lesson 12’s tests live. The lower-left (known, cheap if wrong) is where you may proceed without a new probe. The other two quadrants are traps: known-and-fatal is a constraint you already wrote (or forgot to write); unknown-and-cheap is a curiosity, not a gate. Curiosities eat sprints when nobody names them as curiosities.

The move is FOR stopping the team from testing the assumption they already believe. Teams love confirming the pre-fill model because it is interesting. They avoid testing findability because it is embarrassing if it fails. Mapping is how you make the embarrassment visible before the money is gone. It is also how you stop a critique from wandering into every possible future.

Write assumptions as falsifiable sentences, not as themes. “Findability” is a theme. “A new contractor can open the tax packet from the onboarding email in five minutes without help” is an assumption you can kill. “Pre-fill will delight them” is a mood. “Pre-fill from HRIS reduces incomplete packets by letting them finish in one sitting” is a claim. If you cannot write the fail rule, you do not have an assumption. You have a hope. Hopes do not belong on the map.

Decision rule: circle at most three fatal unknowns. If everything is fatal, you do not have a slice; you have a pile. Rank by “if this is false, the rest of the spend is waste.” That top circle is the only honest reason to delay a ship or a demo. The map is not a deliverable. It is a way to pick the next gate. A beautiful 2×2 that never changes the backlog is stationery.

RAT as a stop/go gate

A riskiest assumption test (RAT) is Lesson 12’s smallest honest test, named as a review gate: we do not spend the next increment until this assumption has a kill/keep signal. RAT is not a workshop canvas you admire. It is a stop/go. If it cannot stop spend, it is not a RAT. It is a sketch of bravery.

Stop means: the next cadence does not build the interesting thing. It builds the test, or it redesigns the thing the test already killed. Go means: this assumption held under the named rule, so you may raise fidelity or widen the slice. Go is not “people were nice.” Go is the fail rule you wrote while you were still honest (Lesson 15 will force you to write that rule before the data). A RAT run after the demo is booked is not a gate. It is an alibi.

If the riskiest assumption is about users, user testing with a task-success measure is the review (Lesson 14). If it is about legality, counsel is the review, not a pretotype. If it is about mechanism, a works-like test is the review. The RAT inherits the kind of prototype from Lesson 11. Do not run a looks-like critique and call it a RAT. A critique can point at the untested assumption. Only a test with a fail rule can clear it.

Decision rule: no ship, no leadership product demo, and no “solidify this slice” while the circled assumption is still a guess. You may still do a stakeholder review of the evidence plan — that is not a demo of the untested product. Confusing those two is how Thursday’s slides eat Monday’s test. If engineering says the slides are already done, the slides are a sunk cost. Sunk costs are not evidence.

Uncertainty: “RAT” is popular product jargon, not a single canonical paper in this syllabus. This pack teaches the gate: stop or go on the fatal unknown, with a signal you named in advance.

Pre-mortem with owners

A pre-mortem assumes the project has already failed, then asks how. It is scheduled pessimism. It catches “we all privately thought this was doomed” before the money is gone. Write the failure stories in the past tense: “It is six months later. Contractors still call HR. The packet shipped. Why did we fail?” Past tense is the trick. It lets people say the unsayable without pretending they are disloyal. Then cluster. Then put owners on the plausible ones.

The move is FOR surfacing the failure the room is too polite to name. It is not FOR venting, and it is not FOR proving you are the smartest skeptic. A pre-mortem that becomes gallows humor with no owners is entertainment. A pre-mortem that produces twelve unowned risks on a slide is a risk register nobody will read. One owner, one next action, one date. If no one will own it, it is not a risk you are managing. It is a story you will retell at the post-mortem as if it were a surprise.

Run it when the spend is about to jump: before you raise fidelity, before you print a launch date, before you promise leadership a demo. Do not run it as a surprise attack in the last five minutes of a happy critique. It is its own short meeting, or a dedicated block after the 35-minute look. Fifteen minutes of writing, ten of clustering, ten of owners is enough. If you need an hour of speeches, you are performing worry.

Decision rule: every plausible cluster gets a name, an owner, and a cheap probe or a constraint. “Legal unreadability kills findability even after we fix the link” is a cluster. Owner: legal + writer, this week. Probe: expert review of the form language (Lesson 14), not another pretty shell. If the cluster is “the vendor goes down on day one,” the owner is operations (Lesson 16), and the probe is a rollback rehearsal, not a brainstorm. Unowned pessimism is a mood. Owned failure stories are cheap.

Steelman the kill case

Steelman is the opposite of a straw man: the strongest fair form of a critic’s view. In review, steelman the case for killing your favorite idea before you keep it. If you cannot, you do not understand the risk; you are defending an identity. Coats, not identities: the idea is a coat you can take off. If taking it off feels like death, you are no longer reviewing.

The move is FOR making keep/kill a decision instead of a loyalty test. The author, or the champion, must be able to say: “The best case for stopping is this: we have no evidence contractors can find the packet; a pre-fill demo will teach leadership the wrong risk; every hour on the model is an hour not spent on the link and the language.” Then they may still keep a different slice. Steelman is not agreement. It is literacy. You may reject the kill case after you can say it. You may not skip saying it.

A steelman has three parts: the critic’s actual claim, the evidence that would make it true, and the cost of ignoring it. “Leadership morale” is not a steelman of the Thursday demo. “Stakeholders need a signal that the job is moving” is the fair form. The answer might be: show them the task-success table, not the pretty shell. That is how steelman changes the spend instead of producing a sarcastic prefix to a straw man (“let me steelman that — it’s dumb”). Sarcasm is a straw man with a costume.

Decision rule: if the kill case is unsayable in the room, your reviews are fake. Kill must be a legal move or the other four moves are costumes. Ask once, out loud: “What would have to be true for us to stop?” If the room laughs, you do not have a team. You have a fan club.

Named end-moves: redesign, solidify, kill, pivot, ship

Review must end in a named move. “Keep thinking” is stalling. Mood is not a move. A parking lot of action items is not a move. Excitement is optional. The five names are the decision rule for this lesson and the hinge into Lessons 15–16:

MoveWhen
RedesignThe job is right; the artifact is wrong. Stay in diamond 2. Same brief, different shape.
SolidifyThe test held; freeze this slice and raise fidelity or Definition of Done. You may spend on craft.
KillThe job or the assumption is dead. Stop spending. Do not rename kill as a pivot to save face.
PivotThe job is still alive; the approach is wrong. Change the bet (Lesson 15). New approach, same job.
ShipEvidence and Definition of Done say this increment can leave the building (Lesson 16).

Redesign is the honest home of most critiques. Something was learned; the job still holds; the screen or the flow is wrong. Solidify is earned. It is not what you say because the author is tired or the slides are booked. Kill is a success of learning when the assumption is false. Pivot is not a nicer word for kill: the job remains. Ship is a mode switch, not a party. If you cannot tell pivot from kill, you will keep spending on a dead job under a livelier noun.

If the meeting ends with “let’s keep thinking,” you did not review. You stalled. The facilitator writes one of the five words on the brief before people stand up. If they cannot, the default is not ship. The default is: no new spend on this artifact until a named move exists. “We need more data” is allowed only if it names the RAT and the stop/go rule. Otherwise it is keep-thinking in a lab coat.

Failure catalog

Critique fails in patterns you can name in the first three minutes. Slide reviews of high-fi work that never saw a user are sales. Experts invited after the launch date is printed are decoration. Steelman used as a sarcastic prefix to a straw man is bullying. Task success defined as “they said it was nice” is not a RAT. Kill is socially impossible, so everything solidifies. The author redesigns live, so the room never finishes a sentence. A senior person stays silent and vetoes later. The brief is a slogan. The question is “what do you think?” The clock is a rumor. The map has twenty sticky notes and no circle.

The other failure is over-protocol: a team that can run I like / I wish / What if forever and never map an assumption. Speech without a gate is still a salon. Map, test, name the move. If spend does not change, it was not a review. A third failure is mixing this meeting with Lesson 14’s three reviews. A critique is the team looking at an artifact against the brief. User testing is a person with the job attempting the job. Stakeholder review asks someone who pays, blocks, or integrates. Expert review asks someone with domain method. Do not invite the paying executive into the 35-minute critique and call their nod a RAT. Do not invite a contractor and their boss into the same look. Power fakes success.

Uncertainty: critique formats vary by school (plus/delta, timed rounds, silent writing first). This pack standardizes on I like / I wish / What if plus a named end-move. It does not claim a single inventor in the syllabus. Pre-mortem is a named field move; this pack requires owners so the pessimism is not entertainment.

Worked example(s)

Problem: The contractor onboarding pretotype (Lessons 11–12) showed 4/5 people could not find the tax packet. Engineering still wants to demo a pre-fill model to leadership on Thursday because the slides are done. The brief’s end state is still “complete the packet unassisted.” There is no 35-minute critique on the calendar — only a “quick look at the deck.”

Steps:

  1. Cancel the deck-as-critique. There is a brief, but the question on the table is the wrong one. The artifact being reviewed is a looks-like of pre-fill. The riskiest assumption was findability. Facilitator writes the real question: “Can a new contractor find and start the packet unassisted?” If Thursday’s meeting cannot look at that, it is not a critique. It is a sales slot.
  2. Run the 35-minute look on the pretotype evidence, not the slides. I like: the pretotype used the real email path, which matches the job. I wish: the first screen named “benefits” and not “tax packet,” against the end state. What if: we test a subject line and a single link this week before any model demo. Author (engineering) stays silent through the round.
  3. Assumption map. “They can find it” was unknown and fatal. It just failed 4/5. “Pre-fill accuracy delights them” is still unknown but not this week’s gate — if they cannot find the form, accuracy never runs. “Leadership needs a signal” is known and not fatal: a table can carry the signal. Circle findability. Do not circle the model.
  4. RAT as stop/go. Stop the pre-fill increment. Go only on a findability redesign. The Thursday product demo is not a test of the circled assumption. It is a looks-like of the wrong risk. Steelman the case for still demoing: “Stakeholders need a signal that onboarding is moving, and the calendar is already sent.” Fair form, not a straw man. The cost of ignoring the kill case: you teach leadership that pre-fill is the product, then you cannot kill it later.
  5. Named move: kill the Thursday product demo as a test. Redesign findability (same job, different artifact). Offer leadership a review of the task-success table instead of a pretty shell. That is a stakeholder review of evidence (Lesson 14), not a ship (Lesson 16).
  6. Pre-mortem the next slice, with owners. Past tense: “We fixed the link and still failed.” Cluster A: the forms are legally unreadable. Owner: legal + writer, this week — expert review, not another pretotype. Cluster B: the email is findable on desktop and invisible on the phone they actually use. Owner: the person who will sit the next five contractors. Cluster C: Thursday’s cancelled demo becomes a hallway veto. Owner: the PM, who sends the task-success table today so the veto has to argue with a number.

Answer / result: Review changed the spend. Pre-fill stays in learning mode with no leadership costume. Findability is the only honest next dollar. That is the only passing grade for a critique.

Summary

  • Critique the artifact against the brief; the author is a witness, not the defendant.
  • Cancel if purpose, end state, or constraints are missing. People will otherwise review a ghost.
  • I like / I wish / What if is a speech protocol with a clock, not a vibe.
  • Map assumptions; treat RAT as stop/go; pre-mortem with owners; steelman the kill case.
  • End with redesign, solidify, kill, pivot, or ship. “Keep thinking” is stalling.
  • If the next spend does not change, it was not a review.
  • Do not mix this 35-minute look with user, stakeholder, or expert rooms (Lesson 14).

Near transfer

A team books “critique” for a high-fidelity onboarding demo. There is no brief on the wall. The VP who championed the demo will sit in. The author plans a twelve-minute walkthrough. What do you cancel, what do you refuse to call this meeting, and what one sentence must exist before you reconvene?

Far transfer

QA testers will look at this Atlas pack. Steelman the kill case for shipping Lesson 13 without a worked example. Then name the end-move you would actually take, and write one owned pre-mortem cluster for “learners finish the quiz and still cannot run a critique.”

Spaced retrieval notes

  • Day 0: run I like / I wish / What if on one artifact you made; force a named end-move in 35 minutes.
  • Day 2: map five assumptions on ignorance × impact; circle at most three fatal unknowns.
  • Day 7: write a half-page pre-mortem of a live project with owners and dates.
  • Day 21: steelman the kill case for your favorite idea in front of the people who loved it, then pick a named move.

Difficulty tiers

  • Novice: cancel without a brief; run the speech protocol; write one of the five end-moves on the page before standing up.
  • Working: assumption map + RAT as stop/go; 35-minute facilitation; pre-mortem with owners.
  • Expert: steelman a kill of your own idea in the room that funded it, and still protect the next test from a demo costume.

Domain scaffolds

Professional reviews: write the brief at the top of the critique agenda. Safety, legal, and money-that-is-not-yours assumptions are expert gates, not pretotypes. Users are not props in a stakeholder show — separate those rooms (Lesson 14). If kill is unsayable in your org, name that as a constraint in the brief; do not pretend the other four moves still work.
