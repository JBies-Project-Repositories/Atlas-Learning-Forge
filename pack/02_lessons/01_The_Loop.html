Atlas of the Build Loop
Dense lesson · professional practice · educational only

The Loop

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish divergent from convergent thinking and name the failure mode of running the wrong one.
  • Classify a piece of work as complicated or complex and pick a loop that matches that class.
  • Walk PDCA, OODA, and Build–Measure–Learn as three named loops with different clocks and jobs.
  • Map Double Diamond and Design Thinking stages onto a single field-guide spine: diverge, converge, test, decide.
  • Refuse a “we already know” skip of the first diamond when the work is still a problem, not a plan.

Prerequisites

  • You have shipped or attempted to ship something (a document, a product slice, a lesson, a process).
  • No project-management credential is required.
  • If the work can harm people (safety, medical, legal, money that is not yours), this lesson does not replace a specialist review.

Lesson Content

Why a loop instead of a longer plan

A plan is a bet that the next hours look like the last hours. That bet is often good: payroll, a known recipe, a statute with a checklist. It is a bad bet when the environment talks back faster than the plan can be rewritten. A loop is a way to keep moving without pretending the map is the territory.

The syllabus names several loops because builders keep rediscovering the same shape: notice, act, see what happened, change the next act. PDCA, OODA, Build–Measure–Learn, Double Diamond, and Design Thinking stages are not competing religions. They are different labels for that shape, tuned to quality, combat time, startups, and design work. If you treat them as brands, you will buy a workshop. If you treat them as moves, you can pick one for the next hour.

The rest of this pack assumes you can name which move you are in. Lesson 2 picks a methodology. Lesson 3 writes intent. None of that helps if you are diverging when the room needed a decision, or converging when the problem was still unnamed.

Divergent vs convergent thinking

Divergent thinking increases the number of live options. It looks messy: How Might We notes, wild sketches, “what if we killed the feature.” Its job is to stop the first plausible idea from becoming the plan. Convergent thinking reduces options against a criterion: constraints, evidence, a definition of done. Its job is to stop exploration from becoming a lifestyle.

The failure mode is running them at the same time in the same mouth. A room that brainstorms while scoring ideas in real time is not diverging; it is holding a quiet trial. A room that “decides” without a criterion is not converging; it is performing confidence. A practical rule: name the mode on the wall. For twenty minutes we only add. Then we only kill, merge, or keep.

These modes are not personality types. The same person should be able to do both. Teams that hire “creatives” for diverge and “operators” for converge often discover that the operators were never shown the problem and the creatives were never shown the constraint. The loop is the handshake, not the org chart.

Complex vs complicated work

Complicated work has many parts, but the parts behave. An expert can decompose it. A checklist can finish it. Building a known house, closing a known month-end, following a published API with tests — these reward expertise, sequence, and a definition of done. Waterfall and a heavy plan are not insults here; they are tools.

Complex work has parts that change each other while you work. Cause and effect are clearer after you act. A new market, a new classroom, a new team habit, a product nobody has used — these reward small probes, not a 40-page Gantt that assumes the users already agreed. The Cynefin-style distinction (complicated vs complex) is the decision rule: if you cannot honestly write the cause-and-effect in advance, you are not in a plan-first domain.

Mislabeling is expensive. Treat complex work as complicated and you will write a specification for a user who does not exist, then call the mismatch “change requests.” Treat complicated work as complex and you will prototype a tax form that already has a statute, then call the delay “learning.” Ask: if I run the same steps next month, do I expect the same result? Yes → complicated. No → complex, so the loop is the product.

PDCA — a quality loop with a named check

PDCA (Plan–Do–Check–Act; also Plan–Do–Study–Act in some quality writing) is a loop for improving a process you can observe. Plan a change small enough to reverse. Do it on a slice. Check against a measure you named in Plan — not against mood. Act: adopt, adjust, or drop.

The “Check” is the part teams skip. They Plan and Do, then Act on vibes. That is not PDCA; that is busywork with a Japanese acronym taped on. The Check must be a comparison: what we predicted vs what happened, on the measure we chose while we were still honest. If you cannot name the measure in Plan, you are not ready to Do.

PDCA fits complicated improvement and the operational half of complex work once you have a probe. It is a poor fit for a brand-new problem that still needs divergence (you would be planning a solution to an unstated job). Use it when a process exists and you want it less wrong next week.

OODA — a tempo loop

OODA (Observe–Orient–Decide–Act) is a loop about time. Observe the environment. Orient — the hard step — by updating your model with the new observation, including what you wished were not true. Decide. Act. Then the environment has already moved, so observe again.

People quote OODA as “move faster than the enemy.” Speed without Orient is just panic with a shorter cycle. Orient is where doctrine, bias, and last week’s success live. If your Orient step is “we always do sprints,” you are not looping; you are replaying. A practical tell: after a surprise, does the team update the model (Orient) or only the task list (Act)?

OODA is at home in competitive, high-feedback settings. It is a poor excuse to skip user research because “we need tempo.” Tempo on a false model is how you ship the wrong thing faster.

Build–Measure–Learn, Double Diamond, and Design Thinking stages

Build–Measure–Learn (Lean Startup) is a loop that treats a product idea as a hypothesis. Build the smallest thing that can falsify it. Measure the behavior you claimed would change. Learn: pivot or persevere. The order in the name is the trap. The intellectual order is Learn (what would change our mind?) → Measure (what signal?) → Build (the smallest test). If you Build first because building is comforting, you have a feature factory with a blog vocabulary.

The Double Diamond (Discover–Define / Develop–Deliver, popularized as a public design model) is two diverge–converge pairs. Diamond 1: explore the problem, then define it. Diamond 2: explore solutions, then deliver one. Skipping diamond 1 is the default corporate injury: a solution diamond aimed at the wrong job.

Design Thinking stages (a common teaching set: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test — labels vary by school) are the same spine with more named doors. Empathize/define are diamond 1. Ideate/prototype/test are diamond 2. They are not a waterfall you must finish in order once. They are a loop: a failed test sends you back to define, not to a blame meeting.

Choosing a loop for the next week

Decision rule, not a personality test:

  1. If a process exists and you can measure it → PDCA on a reversible slice.
  2. If a competitor or environment is moving faster than your meetings → OODA, and protect Orient.
  3. If you have a product bet you could be wrong about → Build–Measure–Learn, with Learn written first.
  4. If you cannot state the problem in one sentence a user would recognize → Double Diamond / Design Thinking, and you are still in diamond 1.

You may use more than one in a month. You may not use all of them in the same meeting. Mixing PDCA “check” language with a brainstorm is how teams fake rigor.

Uncertainty (from a thin syllabus): origin stories for PDCA/OODA/Double Diamond are contested in the literature and in vendor decks. This pack does not settle who “invented” a circle. It teaches the move the name is used for in professional talk.

Worked example(s)

Problem: A three-person team wants to add “AI summaries” to an internal wiki. Leadership asked for a six-month roadmap by Friday. Two engineers already sketched a full architecture. No one has watched a person try to find an answer on the current wiki.

Steps:

  1. Classify the work. The wiki search problem is complex (users, trust, hallucination). The hosting checklist is complicated. Do not put both on one Gantt.
  2. Name the mode. This week is divergent on the job-to-be-done, not convergent on vendors. A six-month roadmap would fake diamond 2.
  3. Pick the loop. This is a product bet → Build–Measure–Learn, nested inside diamond 1. Write the learning: “If we show five people a fake summary on their last failed search, will they trust it enough to stop scrolling?”
  4. Act small. A pretotype (Lesson 5): paper summaries pasted onto screenshots, five sessions, a task-success count. No architecture.
  5. Check. If 0/5 people would rely on it, you did not fail the roadmap. You avoided a six-month Orient-skip.

Answer / result: The Friday artifact is not a roadmap. It is a one-page loop: hypothesis, measure, next probe, and an explicit non-goal (“no vendor selected”). That is commander’s intent for a complex slice, which Lesson 3 will write formally.

Misconception inventory

MisconceptionReality
“Loops mean we never decide.”Convergence is half the loop. A loop without a kill/keep step is stalling.
“PDCA, OODA, and Agile are the same circle.”Same spine, different jobs and clocks. Mixing labels hides which job you skipped.
“If we move faster we are doing OODA.”Speed without Orient is panic. Orient is the scarce step.
“Build–Measure–Learn means build first.”Write the learning and the measure before the build.
“Design Thinking is only for designers.”It is a problem-framing loop. Engineers skip diamond 1 at the same cost.
“Complex work just needs a better Gantt.”A Gantt assumes known cause and effect. Probes come first.
“Divergent and convergent should happen at once to save time.”Simultaneous scoring kills options early and fake-converges.
“We already know the problem, so skip to delivery.”That sentence is the usual way diamond 1 dies. Prove it with a user-recognizable problem statement.

Summary

  • A loop is a way to act when the map is incomplete; a plan is a bet the map is good enough.
  • Diverge and converge are modes, not people. Name which one the room is in.
  • Complicated vs complex decides whether a checklist or a probe is the honest next step.
  • PDCA checks a process; OODA protects Orient; BML falsifies a bet; Double Diamond / Design Thinking frames the problem before the solution.
  • Pick one loop for the week. Do not run a holy war of diagrams.

Practice

Retrieval

  • What is the scarce step in OODA, and what does skipping it look like in a standup?
  • Intellectual order of Build–Measure–Learn?
  • Give one tell that work is complex rather than complicated.
  • What does “Check” add to Plan–Do that vibes do not?

Near transfer

A support team wants to “PDCA” a new chatbot. They have no baseline on ticket time and no agreed wrong-answer cost. What is missing before Do?

Far transfer

You are writing a study pack (this one). Is “which lesson to write next” complicated or complex? Which loop would you use for the next two days, and what would Check look like?

Spaced retrieval notes

  • Day 0: name diverge vs converge on a real meeting you sat in this week.
  • Day 2: classify one current project as complicated or complex in one sentence.
  • Day 7: pick PDCA vs BML for a live task and write the Check/Measure in advance.
  • Day 21: catch one Orient-skip (task list updated, model unchanged).

Difficulty tiers

  • Novice: Use only diverge/converge + complicated/complex + one loop (PDCA or BML).
  • Working: Match all four named loops to jobs without mixing them in one meeting.
  • Expert: Nest a BML probe inside diamond 1 while operations still run PDCA on the known process.

Domain scaffolds

Professional building work: write the loop on the brief. If the artifact is a roadmap, a backlog, or a design file, the first line is the mode (diverge / converge) and the class (complicated / complex). Safety-critical slices stay checklist-first even when the product around them is complex.
